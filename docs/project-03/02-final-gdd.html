1. Introduction

Deepspace Cybercourt is a virtual reality (VR) sports experience set in a futuristic, sci-fi world. This VR game combines elements of tennis and racket gameplay, with players competing against AI (NPC) opponents in a fast-paced VR arena. Players score points by hitting the ball into the opponent’s wall while defending their own wall.

This project aimed to create a VR experience that enhances physical immersion through embodied interaction, responsive physics systems, and replayable competitive gameplay. Players can choose between two types of rackets: a conventional tennis racket and a unique paddle racket. Each offering slightly different gameplay and interaction styles.

The core gameplay interaction included a swinging motion of a racket to hit a tennis ball, rallying with an AI Agent, and playing matches against it. In order to enhance player engagement and immersion through gameplay, SFX, VFX, motion system, and environmental effects were implemented.

2. Prototype Implementation

The prototype of Deepspace Cybercourt was built using Unity version 6000.0.40f1 to present a VR experience with physics-based mechanics. The core gameplay involves players hitting tennis balls towards the opposing NPC’s wall using a racket they chose. The goal is to score against the NPC while preventing the NPC from scoring by letting the ball hit the wall on the player’s side. The players are able to pick up the rackets using grip-based input. This allows the players to swing the racket as they would in real life during gameplay.

The player’s main method of locomotion is through teleportation with the right joystick to blue spots in the environment. The player also has access to another locomotion by using the left joystick to walk. Additionally, although initially unintended, the players are able to double jump using buttons on the controllers.

Multiple feedback systems were implemented within the game to increase immersion and juice, such as particle effects and audio feedback when the ball interacts with the rackets. Other examples of audio include racket swinging and ball bouncing. The game also implements UI systems, for example, tutorial posters and onboarding orbs, to improve the player experience and gameplay readability.

The environment adopted a sci-fi industrial visual style designed to create strong spatial readability while reinforcing immersion and atmosphere. Environmental scale, interaction zones, and object placement were tested in VR to ensure interactions remained comfortable and accessible across different player heights and available play spaces. The enclosed space was refined with changes to the skybox and environment materials to provide an open-space feel and atmosphere.

The NPC opponent functioned as both a gameplay challenge and an interaction facilitator. Rather than acting purely as an enemy, the NPC continuously returns the ball toward the player to sustain the gameplay loop and reinforce the core racket mechanics.

Performance optimisation was also considered throughout development. Simplified geometry, regulated environmental scope, reduced visual complexity, and optimisation-conscious asset usage were used to support stable VR performance and reduce discomfort associated with inconsistent frame rates and glitches.

3. Original Concept to Final Prototype

The original concept for Deepspace Cybercourt was a seated VR arcade tennis experience where players controlled a wheelchair using physical hand movements while aiming and striking the ball with a racket. The intended player experience was to create anticipation, competition, thrill, and a new perspective on sports through embodied wheelchair movement and racket gameplay.

As development progressed, the concept evolved into a broader VR racket-sport prototype set within a sci-fi industrial arena. The final prototype retained the original focus on embodied movement, racket interaction, NPC opposition, and competitive rally gameplay, but several features changed due to technical scope, playtesting outcomes, and implementation constraints.

4. Playtesting and Iteration

Over the development period, three separate playtesting sessions were conducted to evaluate player interaction, onboarding, usability, comfort, immersion, gameplay replayability, and engagement. The playtesters were assessed by observation, prompts to ask during gameplay, and a set of post-game questionnaires with follow-up discussions.

Early playtesting focused on naive interaction discovery. Participants naturally experimented with the environment and quickly understood interactions such as throwing, bouncing, and hitting objects. One of the most significant findings was that players preferred striking and throwing interactions over catching mechanics. This directly influenced the transition toward the final racket-based gameplay loop involving NPC rally gameplay. Additionally, the First phase of playtesting had mainly focused on individual contributions towards the initial build.

Later testing phases demonstrated significant improvements in gameplay engagement and environmental immersion. Players responded positively to the embodied racket interactions, jump and double-jump mechanics, physics-based gameplay, and environmental atmosphere. Several participants naturally incorporated jump “smash” gameplay into rallies without instruction, reinforcing the success of the embodied interaction systems.

However, testing also identified several technical and usability issues. The NPC opponent frequently failed to consistently return the ball during rallies, reducing gameplay challenge and immersion. Additional issues included inconsistent ball physics behaviour, teleportation discomfort, onboarding problems, and environmental readability concerns.

These findings influenced later development decisions involving NPC behaviour systems, ball physics tuning, locomotion comfort, and interaction clarity. The iterative playtesting process reinforced the importance of balancing immersion, comfort, responsiveness, and gameplay readability within VR experiences.

5. Reflection and Development Challenges

With the help of playtesters, the group was able to address pain points found when naive users interacted with our game, which led to significant changes to our prototype.

One of the most difficult challenges was to balance and create a satisfying experience for the player to stay in the flow state. Challenges such as interacting with the NPC, the bounciness of the ball, racket hit swings, and the scoring system.

Another challenge faced when developing the game was the refinement of the NPC behavior. The reason this was a big challenge was that the NPC was one of the main interactions the player had to experience in Deepspace Cybercourt. The difficulty came from adjusting the NPC’s ability to return the ball and the NPC's ability to chase the ball. Previous iterations of the NPC were not able to consistently return the ball, even when it seemed to be capable.

During production, the team also faced a challenge where communication was not as prevalent within the team. While some were active in contributing, the team also had a member who did not interact with the group project since the first week, leading to the production pace dropping in comparison to the average group.

Although there were challenges encountered during the development period, the team pulled through to build the game now known as Deepspace Cybercourt by continuing to build upon it. Some aspects of the original ideas were set aside due to the complexity of implementation as well as development time constraints.

The project reinforced the importance of designing VR experiences around player comfort, embodied interaction, intuitive onboarding, and gameplay clarity rather than mechanical complexity alone.

7. Conclusion

The final prototype successfully demonstrated a functional and immersive VR sports experience focused on embodied interaction, competitive gameplay, and player comfort. Through iterative development and playtesting, the project evolved into a more refined and accessible experience aligned with core VR design principles discussed throughout the unit.

The project highlighted the importance of player-centred design, responsive feedback systems, accessibility-focused interaction design, and iterative refinement within immersive environments. While technical limitations and gameplay bugs remained present in some areas, the prototype effectively achieved its primary goal of creating an engaging, physically interactive, and replayable VR experience.
